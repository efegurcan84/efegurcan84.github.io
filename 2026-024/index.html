  • Editorial: The Enduring Structures and Shifting Grounds of Global Power

    Efe Can Gürcan (ORCID ID: 0000-0002-5415-3163)

    Received: 01 Oct 2026 | Accepted: 10 Oct Aug 2026 | Published online: 12 Oct 2026

    How to cite this article: Gürcan, E. C. (2026). Editorial: The Enduring Structures and Shifting Grounds of Global Power. Global Geopolitics, 1(4), 459-461.

    DOI: 10.64901/29778271.2026.024

    The fourth issue of Global Geopolitics brings the journal’s inaugural volume to a close by exploring how established structures of power and dependency shape the possibilities for adaptation and agency in a changing international order. Across different geographical and institutional contexts, the five contributions examine how states and societies navigate structural constraints, respond to emerging challenges, and seek to redefine their positions within existing arrangements. They reveal a central tension in contemporary geopolitics: while established hierarchies, dependencies, and strategic practices continue to shape global relations, their persistence also generates pressures for institutional adaptation, alternative forms of agency, and new understandings of sovereignty and power.

    Opening the issue, Rosemary Achiaa Asamoah and Janaka Jayawickrama’s “Geocultural Epistemologies of Women’s Empowerment: Reclaiming the Legacy of African Philosophy” challenges the dominance of Global North epistemologies in conceptualizing women’s empowerment. Challenging the dominance of Global North frameworks, the authors draw on Ubuntu, Akan philosophy, and communalism to advance a relational conception of empowerment grounded in indigenous African philosophical traditions. Their contribution demonstrates how alternative epistemologies can challenge established hierarchies of knowledge and power while providing conceptual foundations for more contextually grounded approaches to gender equality and women’s agency.

    The following two contributions examine how established hierarchies of geopolitical and economic dependency constrain states’ strategic agency and shape their efforts to pursue greater autonomy. In “Geopolitical Leverage: Ethiopia’s Red Sea Imperatives in the Era of BRICS,” Yehualaset Agiz situates Ethiopia’s pursuit of maritime access within regional rivalries and the opportunities and constraints associated with an emerging multipolar order. The analysis underscores the importance of diplomatic negotiation and regional cooperation in addressing Ethiopia’s structural vulnerabilities. Turning to Europe, “The Role of the Ukraine Conflict on EU Energy Policy: Seeking Solutions to Energy Dependency” examines how the conflict has reshaped the European Union’s approach to energy security, accelerating a shift toward diversification, resilience, renewable energy, and strategic autonomy.

    The final two contributions explore how established frameworks of security and sovereignty confront changing geopolitical and environmental conditions, highlighting the interplay between continuity and adaptation. In “Enduring Deterrence from the Skies: The Tu-95 and B-52 in Contemporary Geopolitics,” Matheus F. A. S. Lima and Daniel A. de Azevedo investigate the continued strategic relevance of Cold War-era bombers, demonstrating how established military technologies remain instruments of deterrence, power projection, and geopolitical signaling. In turn, Christa Joy’s “Statehood, Nationality, and Sovereignty Under Climate Change: The Case of Tuvalu” examines how climate-induced territorial loss challenges conventional understandings of statehood and sovereignty. Drawing on international legal doctrine and Tuvalu’s diplomatic responses, particularly the 2023 Falepili Union Treaty, the article explores how existing legal frameworks can adapt to preserve state continuity amid territorial loss and population displacement.

    The articles in this issue show that established hierarchies and dependencies do not simply determine the course of international relations, but also shape the conditions under which states and societies seek alternatives, negotiate constraints, and adapt to changing circumstances. Understanding these processes requires attention to both the persistence of existing power structures and the possibilities for their transformation. As Global Geopolitics completes its inaugural volume, we remain committed to advancing critical, interdisciplinary, and geographically inclusive scholarship that contributes to these debates. We are grateful to our authors, reviewers, and editorial board members for their contributions to the journal’s first year and look forward to building on this foundation in the volumes ahead.
